# World Trip

Fly anywhere in the world from Aruna. You hear the captain and the engines, land to the
local time and weather, are greeted in the local language by a native voice, and listen to
local radio while you ask about the city or learn a phrase. It needs Hariku 2.9 or newer
and an internet connection.

## Getting started

Install World Trip from the Extension Store. Open Aruna, Hariku's command bar, with
Ctrl+Alt+Backspace, type "take me to Tokyo" and press Enter. With the Voice Control
extension you can say it instead.

Set your main place in Preferences, Places. World Trip flies from there: the captain gives
the distance and flight time, and on arrival you hear how far ahead of or behind home the
local time is. Without a main place the trip still works, without those parts.

## Taking a trip

Tell Aruna where to go:

- "take me to Tokyo", "fly to Seoul", "visit New York", "travel to Istanbul"
- "bawa aku ke Tokyo", "terbang ke Paris", "jalan-jalan ke Istanbul"
- "take me to Japan": a country lands in its capital or its best-known city, here Tokyo.
- "take me anywhere", "surprise me" or "bawa aku ke mana saja": a surprise city, chosen
  from a list of well-known places, never the same one twice in a row.

A new trip replaces the one you are on. If the place is less than about 30 kilometres from
your main place, Aruna tells you that you're already there. A sentence with a date or
time, such as "take me to the doctor tomorrow at 9", is a reminder, not a trip.

## The flight

1. A cabin chime, then the captain's announcement, for example: "Welcome aboard Hariku
  Air. Flight from Batam to Tokyo, about 5,300 kilometres, roughly 7 hours. Please fasten
  your seat belt." A surprise trip names the city here.
2. The engines.

To skip straight to the arrival, press any key or say "skip". You can turn the
announcement and the engines off in Settings.

## Arriving

1. A chime, then the city, its local time and part of the day, how far ahead of or behind
  home it is, and the weather: "Welcome to Tokyo, Japan. Local time 21:30, Friday night, 2
  hours ahead of Batam. 18 degrees, drizzle."
2. A greeting for that time of day, with "welcome to Tokyo", in the local language and
  spoken by a native voice ("Konbanwa! Tōkyō e yōkoso!"), then what it means in your
  language. When people there speak your own language, you just hear the greeting.
3. Local radio starts playing quietly (see Local radio).

On your first three trips, Hariku ends the arrival with a few things you can say next.

Everything the trip says also shows in Aruna's "Last result". You can close Aruna: the
trip goes on.

## Things to ask during a trip

- "tell me about this city" or "ceritakan tentang kota ini": a few sentences from
  Wikipedia, in your language when Wikipedia has them, else from English Wikipedia. Name
  the city or its country instead ("tell me about Japan") to hear about that.
- "teach me a phrase" or "ajari aku satu kalimat": an everyday phrase in the native voice,
  then how to say it and what it means. There are eight: thank you, excuse me, how much is
  this, delicious, where is the toilet, nice to meet you, goodbye and cheers. Each time
  you get a different one until you have heard them all. Phrases come in 31 languages.
- "what time is it there" or "jam berapa di sana": the local time and the difference from
  home.
- "where am I" or "di mana aku": the city, how far it is from home, the local time and the
  radio station.

## Local radio

On arrival, a live station from the city, or else from the country, plays quietly. It
turns itself down while Hariku speaks, and Hariku says which station it is. If a station
doesn't play, the next one is tried.

- "next station" or "ganti stasiun": another station.
- "radio louder" or "keraskan", "radio quieter" or "kecilkan": 10 percent up or down. The
  new volume is kept for the next trips. With no radio playing, these change Hariku's own
  volume instead.
- "which station is this" or "stasiun apa ini".

The radio stops when you go home, when a new trip starts and when Hariku closes. It needs
Windows Media Foundation, which Windows N editions only have with the Media Feature Pack;
Hariku tells you if it's missing.

## Native voices

Greetings and phrases are spoken by a voice of that language from Hariku Voice. The Edge
Voices extension (from the Extension Store) has one for nearly every language, so it is
tried first; then Piper and Windows voices, when they have one. Without a native voice,
the words are read in your own voice, as they sound in Latin letters, followed by their
meaning. If you don't have Edge Voices, Hariku suggests it once.

## Going home

Say "go home", "take me home" or "pulang". The radio stops and the captain heads home.
With the departure announcement and the engine sound on, you hear the engines (any key or
"skip" skips them). Then a chime, the time at home and "Thank you for flying Hariku Air."
Going home while Hariku is still looking the place up simply cancels the trip.

## Settings

Open Preferences (Ctrl+P in Hariku's window) and choose World Trip.

- Announce the departure (the captain's announcement; any key skips it). Turn it off to
  land at once.
- Engine sound on take-off (only with the departure announcement on).
- Play local radio at the destination.
- Radio volume: a slider, 35 by default.
- Speak greetings and phrases in native voices. Turn it off to hear them in your own
  voice.
- Native voice for each language: which voice each of the 31 languages would use, or that
  it has none yet.

The page also lists what you can say, and where the radio, the city facts and the weather
come from.

## Keys and commands

None of these has a key by default. You can give each one a key in Preferences, Input
Gestures, where they are under "World Trip"; tick "Make this shortcut Global" so the key
works in every window. What you can say to Aruna is in quotes.

- World Trip to a surprise city: "take me anywhere", "surprise me", "bawa aku ke mana
  saja", "kejutkan aku".
- Skip the World Trip flight: "skip", "skip the flight", "lewati", "langsung mendarat".
- Tell me about the city of your World Trip: "tell me about this city", "ceritakan tentang
  kota ini".
- Teach me a phrase in the local language: "teach me a phrase", "another phrase", "ajari
  aku satu kalimat".
- What time is it there on your World Trip: "what time is it there", "local time", "jam
  berapa di sana", "waktu setempat".
- Where am I on my World Trip: "where am I", "where are we", "di mana aku", "lokasiku".
- Next radio station on your World Trip: "next station", "change station", "ganti
  stasiun", "stasiun lain".
- Radio louder on your World Trip: "radio louder", "turn the radio up", "keraskan".
- Radio quieter on your World Trip: "radio quieter", "turn the radio down", "kecilkan".
- Which radio station is playing on your World Trip: "which station is this", "stasiun apa
  ini".
- Fly home from your World Trip: "go home", "take me home", "fly home", "pulang", "ayo
  pulang".

A trip itself starts with "take me to", "fly to", "visit", "travel to", "trip to", "bawa
aku ke", "terbang ke", "jalan-jalan ke", "kunjungi" or "liburan ke", followed by the
place.

## Privacy

World Trip goes online only when you take a trip or ask something during one, and it only
sends the destination, never anything about you:

- The place you asked for, as you typed or said it, goes to Open-Meteo's place search. The
  weather request sends the destination's point, rounded to about 1 kilometre, to
  Open-Meteo.
- The radio asks the free Radio Browser directory for stations, with the destination's
  country code and rounded point, and tells it which station started playing, as the
  directory asks. The station itself streams from its own server, which sees your IP
  address like any radio player.
- "Tell me about this city" sends the city's or country's name to Wikipedia.
- A native voice from Edge Voices sends the greeting or phrase to Microsoft's speech
  service. Piper and Windows voices speak on your computer.

The distance and flight time are worked out on your computer: your places never leave it.
Station lists and Wikipedia summaries are kept on your computer for a day and a week, with
your settings and the number of trips you took. "Any key skips the flight" only asks
Windows when a key was last pressed, during the flight; it never records which keys.
